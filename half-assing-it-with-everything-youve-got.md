# Half-assing it with everything you've got

> **One-line takeaway:** Both coasting and maximal effort are wasted motion — the correct move is to work out which point on the quality line your actual goals imply, deploy full strength to reach it as efficiently as possible, and then stop.

## Summary

An essay from the *Replacing Guilt* series, whose stated project is to remove guilt and shame as primary motivators and replace them with something more durable. It opens with a college student facing a paper whose quality rises with effort invested. Education, the essay argues, implicitly teaches that your preferences point ever rightward along that quality line, and people split into two camps in response: **slackers**, who reject the premise that quality equals preference and therefore withhold effort entirely, and **tryers**, who accept the premise and push rightward without asking why.

Both are described as wasting motion. The slacker is right that the quality line is not their preference curve, but responds by refusing to deploy full strength at all. The tryer deploys full strength at a target that was drawn by someone else. The proposed third path is to work out what the goal actually implies — pass, ace, learn the material, some weighted combination — which picks out a specific target point, and then hit that point with minimum expenditure. Hence the title: half-ass everything, with everything you've got.

The essay then handles the obvious objection (some things are too important to half-ass) by separating two claims that usually travel together: the rule "aim at your target, no higher" always holds, and the target itself can be arbitrarily high — sometimes unattainably so, in which case pushing rightward genuinely is correct. What is never correct is inferring the target from whatever quality line the environment happens to display.

## Key Contributions

- **The quality line vs. the preference curve.** The central distinction. A quality line is the externally legible metric an environment advertises (grades, publication venue, polish). A preference curve is what you actually want. They are only ever tangentially related, and conflating them is the shared error behind both failure modes.
- **A three-way taxonomy where the usual framing has two.** Slacker / tryer is the culturally available dichotomy, and it is loaded — tryers are treated as virtuous and slackers as deficient. The essay's move is to notice that the axis itself is wrong: the interesting variable is not *how much* strength you deploy but *what target* you deploy it at. The third position — full strength aimed at a deliberately middling target — is described as rare and difficult, not as a compromise between the other two.
- **Scarcity as the grounding argument.** "Negentropy is scarce" is the load-bearing premise. Effort, time, and attention are finite in a way that makes overshooting a target genuinely costly rather than merely inelegant. The physics vocabulary is doing rhetorical work: it frames the constraint as a fact rather than a preference.
- **Redirecting perfectionism rather than suppressing it.** Aimed at readers who cannot simply stop caring: be a perfectionist about the *process* — strategy selection, abandoning sunk costs, killing darlings, noticing when you are done — instead of about the artifact.
- **A reframing of guilt.** Guilt about failing to adopt an externally supplied quality line is treated as noise and rejected outright. Guilt about missing targets you actually chose is retained as the only kind worth having, and is the one the rest of the series proposes to address.

## Method

Not an empirical paper. The argument is a worked conceptual distinction plus a series of anticipated objections, each answered in turn: the "some things matter too much" objection (answered by decoupling the rule from the height of the target), the slacker objection that minimum-effort is itself boring (answered by: then abandon the goal entirely — the essay is willing to say drop the class), and the tryer objection that perfectionism is not voluntary (answered by redirecting it onto process).

One structural detail worth noting: the essay explicitly disclaims the cynical reading. It is not arguing for scraping by, and not arguing that the target should be the worst outcome you would grudgingly accept. It also flags the planning fallacy as a reason to build margin into a chosen target — aim at a D and you may land an F.

## Results

No results in the empirical sense. The claim to evaluate is descriptive: that most people operate in only two modes, and that the middle target pursued at full strength is rare. That claim is intuitive but untested, and the essay offers no evidence for it beyond illustration.

## Discussion Notes

**The asymmetry that makes this harder than it sounds.** The essay makes a point that is easy to skim past: minimising wasted motion is a *strictly harder* optimisation problem than pushing rightward. When you are maximising quality, every mistake is recoverable with more time — you can always keep editing. When you are minimising effort, mistakes are irrevocable, because time cannot be un-wasted. The honest consequence, which the essay states rather than hides, is that converting from tryer to whole-assed-half-asser can *increase* guilt, since every misspent hour is now legibly a failure. The essay's answer to this is essentially a promissory note to later posts in the series, which is the weakest part of the argument as it stands.

**The recursion is the sharpest part, and the essay under-develops it.** The framework says: find your target, ignore the environment's quality line. But a target you sincerely believe is your own can itself be an intermediate metric standing in for something else. If the real preference is "do work in this field that matters," then a specific credential, prize, or venue is a *proxy* for that — and treating it as terminal reproduces exactly the tryer error one level up, with the quality line now internalised rather than imposed. This suggests the diagnostic has to be applied recursively: for each target, ask what it is a proxy for, until you reach something that is not instrumental to anything else. The corollary for guilt is direct — failing to hit a proxy metric is not failing your goal, so that guilt is the noisy kind the essay tells you to discard, even though it feels like the legitimate kind.

**A descriptive inversion of the prescriptive frame.** The essay is prescriptive: work out your target, then aim at it. But the split it describes can also be read as a *readout*. The domains where a person spontaneously deploys full strength — with no external quality line demanding it, and no sense of effort — are evidence about where their preference curve is actually steep. The essay treats "overachieve at work, coast in the relationship" as an illustration of the dichotomy's arbitrariness; read the other way, that pattern is data about what someone values, whether or not they endorse it on reflection. The prescriptive and descriptive readings can conflict, and when they do it is not obvious which should win: an effortless full-strength commitment might be a discovered preference, or it might be a compulsion wearing the costume of one. The essay does not distinguish these.

**"Negentropy" as rhetoric.** The word is doing more than "effort is limited" would. In thermodynamics, entropy is disorder that increases spontaneously; negentropy is the ordered capacity that resists it. Borrowing the term frames finite attention as a physical law rather than a personal shortcoming, which is precisely what a reader motivated by guilt needs to hear. Whether the physics carries any actual argumentative weight is doubtful — it is an analogy, not a derivation.

**Where I think it is weakest.** The essay assumes targets are legible enough to identify in advance. In practice, the reason people default to "push rightward" is often that the target genuinely is unknown — you cannot compute the minimum sufficient effort for a competitive selection process whose bar is not published, or for research whose value is only clear afterwards. Under that uncertainty, overshooting is a rational hedge, not confusion. The essay's own planning-fallacy caveat gestures at this without engaging it: "leave yourself comfortable margins" is exactly the concession that the target is not knowable, and once margins are large the practical difference between whole-assed half-assing and just trying hard narrows considerably.

## Connections

- [[inverse-scaling-test-time-compute]] — a structural rhyme rather than a shared mechanism, and worth naming as such. That paper shows that more test-time deliberation is not monotonically better: past a point, additional reasoning degrades performance through distraction and constraint loss. The essay makes the analogous claim for human effort — the quality achieved is not the quantity being optimised, so more effort past the target is at best neutral and at worst actively counterproductive. Both undercut the intuition that additional compute or additional effort is always weakly beneficial. The analogy should not be pushed further than that: the mechanisms are unrelated, and one is empirical while the other is conceptual.
- [[motivation-and-effort]] — topic thread for effort allocation, target selection, and the motivational structures behind sustained research work.

## Open Questions

- **How do you set a target when the bar is genuinely unpublished?** The framework needs a known target to aim at. Selection processes, research value, and most real work do not supply one. Is the right move to estimate the bar and add margin (in which case the framework collapses toward "try hard, but stop eventually), or to substitute a different, knowable target?
- **How far does the recursion go, and how do you know when to stop?** Every target can be interrogated as a proxy for something further up. There is presumably a level at which the question stops being productive, but the essay gives no stopping criterion.
- **Is effortless full-strength engagement evidence of a preference, or of a compulsion?** The descriptive reading above needs a way to tell these apart. Without one, "notice where you naturally give everything" is unreliable as a diagnostic.
- **Does the guilt substitution actually hold empirically?** The essay's causal claim — that guilt-based motivation induces akrasia and drains motivation over long horizons — is asserted from observation of the author's social circle. It is a testable claim and a fairly central one to the whole series.
