# Motivation and Effort Allocation

> **Thread summary:** How finite effort gets aimed — at which targets, under which motivational structure, and by what criterion you decide a piece of work is finished. Distinct from the technical threads: the object of study here is the researcher's own allocation problem rather than a model's.

## Overview

Research work is unusually badly served by the default "do your best" norm. There is no grade, the bar for most selection processes is unpublished, and the quality of a piece of work is often only legible in retrospect. Under those conditions the question "when do I stop?" has no environmental answer, so it gets answered by whatever motivational structure is running underneath — frequently guilt, deadline pressure, or comparison against peers.

This thread collects material on that allocation problem: how to pick a target when the environment supplies a misleading one, what motivational substrate is durable over multi-year horizons, and how to distinguish work that serves a goal from work that merely resembles it.

It is a small thread so far, seeded by one essay.

## Key Papers

1. **[[half-assing-it-with-everything-youve-got]]** (Soares, 2015) — separates the externally advertised *quality line* from your actual *preference curve*, and argues that both coasting and maximal effort waste motion. The prescription is full strength aimed at a deliberately chosen target. Also contains the guilt reframe: guilt about missing your own targets is the only kind worth keeping.

## Emerging Themes

- **The target, not the intensity, is the interesting variable.** The culturally available axis (slacker ↔ tryer) measures how hard you push. The more useful axis is what you are pushing toward, and whether you chose it.
- **Proxy targets recur one level up.** A target adopted sincerely can still be an intermediate metric for something further along. Credentials, venues, and acceptance decisions are the standard examples in a research context. The diagnostic has to be applied recursively or it stops one step short.
- **Unknown bars break the framework.** Aiming at a target presupposes knowing where it is. Competitive selection and open-ended research usually do not publish one, which is a large part of why overshooting is the common default.

## Our Take

The framework's real value is diagnostic rather than prescriptive. Its prescription ("identify your target, hit it, stop") assumes a legibility that research work rarely has. What survives that objection is the question it forces: for any given piece of work, *whose* line am I moving along, and what is it a proxy for? That question is answerable even when the target is not.

The second thing worth carrying forward is the inversion. Where you spontaneously deploy full strength — absent any external demand — is evidence about your preference curve, not just a quirk to be corrected. That reading is in tension with the essay's own prescriptive frame, and the tension is unresolved.

## Open Questions

- What replaces "aim at the target" when the bar is genuinely unpublished? Estimate-plus-margin collapses back toward ordinary effort maximisation.
- Is there a stopping criterion for the proxy-target recursion, or does it bottom out only in terminal values?
- Effortless full engagement: discovered preference, or compulsion? The descriptive reading needs a way to tell them apart before it can be trusted as a signal.
- Is guilt-based motivation actually worse over long horizons, as the *Replacing Guilt* series assumes? The claim is central and empirically untested.
